EU CBAM in 2026–27: the data importers need from their suppliers

The EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism has been in its definitive phase since 1 January 2026. The hard part is not the declaration. It is getting installation-level emissions data from producers outside the EU, in a form a verifier will accept.

The short answer

If you import more than 50 tonnes a year of in-scope iron and steel, aluminium, cement or fertiliser goods into the EU, or any in-scope hydrogen or electricity, you need to be an authorised CBAM declarant. You file an annual CBAM declaration and surrender CBAM certificates. The first declaration, for goods imported in 2026, is due by 30 September 2027. To use real emissions instead of conservative default values, you need verified data from the installations that made the goods.

Who does what

CBAM puts the legal obligation on the EU side and the data burden on the producer side. Keep the two apart in your process, or supplier requests turn into compliance advice.

  • Authorised CBAM declarant (EU importer or representative). Files the annual declaration in the CBAM Registry, buys and surrenders certificates, and keeps the records.
  • Operator of the installation outside the EU. Monitors the installation's emissions and produces the embedded-emissions data for each good it sells. Many operators supply several EU customers from the same data set.
  • Accredited verifier. Verifies the operator's emissions data where actual values are used.
  • Your trader or distributor, if there is one. Often sits between importer and producer, and is often where data requests stall.

Actual values or default values

Where you don't have reliable actual data, you can use default values published by the Commission. They are designed to be conservative, so for an efficient producer they usually overstate emissions and the importer pays for certificates it would not otherwise need.

Using actual values means the producer's data must be verified by an accredited verifier. In practice, the cost of measuring and verifying is weighed against the certificates it saves. Track both figures side by side for each good, so the decision is based on numbers, not guesses.

What to ask your suppliers for

Send one structured request per installation, not per shipment. A good request asks for:

  1. The installation's identity and location, and which of your products it makes.
  2. The production route and the reporting period the figures cover.
  3. Specific direct embedded emissions per tonne for each CN code, and indirect emissions for cement and fertiliser goods.
  4. Precursors used, with their embedded emissions and where they came from.
  5. The verification report, or the date verification is expected.
  6. Any carbon price already paid in the country of origin, with proof.
  7. A named contact who can answer questions from your verifier.

Ask early. Producers outside the EU, including many in India, Turkey and China, are fielding the same request from several buyers at once. The ones with an organised data set answer first.
